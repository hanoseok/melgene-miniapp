Pumpkin carving online: how to design your own jack-o'-lantern

Updated October 9, 2026

No knife, no mess, no slimy hands. This guide shows how the online pumpkin carving tool works, how to give your pumpkin a face with real personality, and how to save or share the result. It also covers the history of the jack-o'-lantern and a few safety tips for carving a real one.

What the tool is and how it works

The Jack-o'-Lantern Maker is a digital pumpkin you can customize piece by piece. You choose the overall shape and color of the pumpkin, then pick the eyes, nose, mouth and stem, and finish with a few extras. Altogether there are dozens of parts to combine, so two pumpkins rarely come out the same.

Two switches change the whole mood. The candle fills the carved parts with a warm glow, like a real flame inside the pumpkin, and the night sky swaps the background between a starry evening and a soft, light scene. You can also give your pumpkin a name of up to twenty characters, which appears with the finished picture.

Step by step

Most people finish in a minute or two, but there is no timer, so take as long as you like.

  • Open the tabs for shape, color, eyes, nose, mouth, stem and extras, and tap an option in each.
  • Tap the pumpkin itself to jump to the next option for the part you are editing.
  • Press Random if you want a surprise, then fine-tune whatever you like or dislike.
  • Try the candle and the night sky, give the pumpkin a name, and press done.

Tips for a face with personality

Start with the eyes, because they carry most of the emotion. Wide, round eyes read as surprised or friendly, narrow slanted ones look sly or fierce, and mismatched shapes feel silly. Once the eyes set the mood, choose a mouth that supports it or deliberately contradicts it. A sweet face with a toothy grin is spooky in a fun way.

Pay attention to contrast and balance. A very busy face with every extra turned on can look cluttered, while a simple face with two eyes, a small nose and one big smile is easy to read from far away, which is exactly what makes real jack-o'-lanterns look good on a porch. The same idea works on screen.

Color matters too. The classic orange feels traditional, but trying an unusual color with the candle on can produce a surprisingly striking glow. If you cannot decide, build two versions and compare them before you finish.

Where jack-o'-lanterns come from

Carving lanterns for the autumn season has a long history in Ireland and Scotland, where people traditionally hollowed out turnips and other root vegetables and put a light inside. The name is tied to a folk tale about a character called Jack, and the word was also used in older English for a night watchman carrying a lantern.

When Irish and Scottish immigrants reached North America, they found pumpkins growing in abundance. Pumpkins are large, soft inside and much easier to carve than turnips, so they gradually became the standard material. Over time, carving a pumpkin turned into one of the most recognizable parts of Halloween, and today it is a family activity in many countries around the world.

Saving and sharing your pumpkin

When you are happy with the design, you can save it as an image. On a phone the image opens in the share sheet so you can keep it in your photos or send it to a friend, and on a computer it downloads as a picture file. It is a good size for wallpapers, profile pictures and party invitations.

You can also copy a share link. The whole design, including its name, is packed into the link itself, so nothing is stored on a server. Whoever opens the link sees exactly the same pumpkin and can then carve their own. Because the name is part of the link, avoid typing personal information into it.

Make a real one safely

If your digital pumpkin inspires you to carve a real one, let the design be your template. Sketch it on paper first, and carve it a few days before Halloween because cut pumpkins start to soften. Children should only draw the design and scoop the inside, while an adult handles the knife. A battery-powered LED candle is a safer light source than an open flame and keeps the pumpkin from cooking inside.
