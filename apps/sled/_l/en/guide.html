Sled game: how to play Sled Race and go further down the hill

Updated November 16, 2026

Sled Race is a quick downhill game for phones, tablets and computers. You ride a little red sled down a snowy hill that never seems to end, weaving between pine trees, rocks and snowmen while picking up flags and gift boxes along the way. A run lasts ninety seconds, or ends early after three bumps, and the hill gets faster the longer you stay on it. This guide covers the controls, what everything on the slope does, habits that lead to bigger scores and a bit of the real history behind sledding.

How to play

Press the start button and the hill appears with your sled near the top of the picture. Nothing moves until your first touch or key press, so you can take a breath before you push off. From then on the sled slides downhill by itself and the slope scrolls towards you. Your only job is steering.

On a phone or tablet, press and hold the left half of the hill to lean left, or the right half to lean right. If you prefer finer control, put your finger down and drag sideways: the sled follows your finger and stops where you stop. On a computer, hold the left and right arrow keys, or A and D. Let go and the sled straightens out and runs straight down.

  • A full run lasts 90 seconds and ends at a checkered finish line.
  • Trees, rocks and snowmen each cost one heart; three bumps end the run early.
  • Distance always adds to your score, and the sled speeds up over time.
  • Flags, gift boxes and the rare golden star add bonus points.
  • Switching tabs or locking your phone pauses the run and the clock.

What you will meet on the slope

Obstacles are easy to recognise: dark green pine trees, grey rocks with a cap of snow and cheerful snowmen. Touching one knocks off a heart and slows the sled down for a moment. Right after a bump the sled blinks, and while it blinks nothing can hurt it, which gives you a second to get back on line.

Small red flags are worth a few points each, colourful gift boxes are worth more, and every now and then a glowing golden star appears that is worth far more than either. Light blue ramps with white arrows launch the sled into the air when you ride straight over them. While you are airborne you sail over trees and rocks without a scratch, and the jump itself earns a bonus. There is always a way through each row of obstacles, even when the hill gets crowded, so a clear path is there if you look for it.

Tips for a higher score

Because distance keeps adding up, the single most important thing is to stay on the hill until the finish line. Every heart you keep also keeps your speed up, since each bump slows the sled. Bonus items matter, but chasing a gift into a tree usually costs more than it earns.

  • Look further down the slope, not at the sled; the next gap matters more than the current one.
  • Make small, early corrections instead of last-second swerves.
  • Dragging gives precise control when the trees get close together.
  • Head for ramps whenever they are near your path; the airtime is free safety and free points.
  • Use the blinking moment after a bump to cut through a crowded patch.
  • Let the edge gifts go late in the run, when the hill is fastest.

A short history of sledding

Sleds are among the oldest vehicles people have built. Long before wheels were practical in snowy country, flat sledges pulled by people or animals carried food, firewood and belongings across ice and snow. The word toboggan reaches English through Canadian French from an Algonquian word for a flat, curved-front sled made of thin boards.

Sliding downhill just for fun became a craze in the nineteenth century, especially in Alpine resorts. In 1883 visitors in Davos, Switzerland, held one of the first organised sled races, from Davos down to the village of Klosters. A year later the famous Cresta Run was built in nearby St. Moritz for riders who lay head first on their sleds. Those experiments grew into today's Olympic sliding sports: bobsleigh has been part of the Winter Games since the very first ones in 1924, luge joined in 1964, and skeleton returned for good in 2002. In parks and back gardens, of course, a plastic sled and a gentle slope are still all anyone needs.

Sledding with friends, on screen and on snow

Sled Race is easy to share. Pass one phone around and give everyone a single run, or each play on your own device and compare scores, distance and jumps at the end. Sending your result with the share button is a simple way to start a friendly rivalry. If the real snow is falling outside, a few common-sense habits keep an afternoon on the sledding hill fun for everyone.

  • Pick a slope with a long, flat run-out and no roads, fences or ponds at the bottom.
  • Sit facing forward with your feet first rather than lying head first.
  • Go down one at a time and walk back up along the side of the hill.
  • Wear a helmet, gloves and warm layers, and stop when it gets dark.
