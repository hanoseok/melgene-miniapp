Enneagram test guide: nine types, one circle and what drives you

Updated November 4, 2026

Why do some people charge straight at a problem while others step back to think, or check first how everyone else feels? The Enneagram is a popular personality model that describes nine basic ways people see the world, each built around a different core motivation. The Enneagram Test turns that idea into a short, friendly quiz made of twelve everyday situations. It is made for fun and self-reflection, so treat your result as a mirror to think with, not a label to live by.

What the Enneagram is

The word comes from the Greek ennea, meaning nine, and gramma, meaning something drawn or written. The Enneagram symbol is a circle with nine evenly spaced points, joined inside by a triangle and a six-sided figure. Each point stands for one personality type, and the lines between the points describe how a person may change under stress or as they grow.

What makes the Enneagram different from many other personality systems is its focus on motivation rather than behavior. Two people can both work late every night, one because they want to do things right and another because they want to be admired or to feel safe. The Enneagram asks why you do what you do: what you want most deeply, and what you try hardest to avoid.

A short history of the symbol and the types

The nine-pointed figure was introduced to Western audiences in the early twentieth century by the teacher George Gurdjieff, who used it as a symbol of process and change rather than of personality. In the 1960s the Bolivian-born teacher Oscar Ichazo connected the points to patterns of human character, and in the 1970s the Chilean psychiatrist Claudio Naranjo linked them with ideas from modern psychology and taught them in California.

From there the model spread through books and workshops. Authors such as Helen Palmer, and Don Richard Riso with Russ Hudson, wrote detailed descriptions of each type and developed longer questionnaires, including the Riso-Hudson Enneagram Type Indicator, often called the RHETI. Today the Enneagram is used in coaching, team building and personal growth. It is worth knowing that scientific research on it is still limited, and psychologists generally rely on other, better-tested models when they measure personality.

How this quiz works: three centers and three stances

Enneagram teachers group the nine types in several ways. One of the best known is the three centers of intelligence. Some people tend to react first from the body and their instincts, some from the heart and their feelings about themselves and others, and some from the head, by thinking, analyzing and planning ahead. Every type belongs to one of these three centers.

A second grouping, often linked to the psychoanalyst Karen Horney, describes three ways people go after what they need. Some move forward and push for it, some try to do what is expected and right, and some step back into their own inner world. Each type also belongs to exactly one of these three stances, and no two types share the same center and the same stance.

This quiz uses that structure in a simple way. Half of the questions point to a center and half point to a stance, and your type is where the two meet. The scoring was checked so that random answers would land on every type about equally often, and the same answers always give the same result. Your result page also shows where your answers leaned and the direction in which your type is said to grow.

How to get the most honest result

The test has twelve short questions and takes about two minutes. There are no right or wrong answers, and no type is better than another. The more honest you are, the more useful the reflection will be.

  • Answer with your first instinct, the way you usually react, not the way you wish you reacted.
  • Think about your ordinary, relaxed self rather than how you act at work or on a stressful day.
  • If two options both fit, pick the one that sounds most like what you want, not just what you do.
  • Use the back arrow if you change your mind; nothing is saved until the end.
  • If the result surprises you, retake the test later or read about the types next to yours on the circle.

Just for fun: what this test is and is not

This quiz is entertainment and a prompt for self-reflection. It is not an official Enneagram assessment such as the RHETI, it has not been validated by researchers, and it cannot diagnose anything. Twelve questions can only point you toward a possible type. Many people recognize parts of themselves in two or three types, and Enneagram teachers usually encourage you to find your own type by reading, reflecting and talking with people who know you well.

No type is good or bad, healthy or unhealthy. Each one comes with real gifts and typical blind spots, and anyone can grow. If you are going through a hard time, or personality questions start to feel stressful rather than fun, a counselor or psychologist can offer far more support than any online quiz.

Sharing your result with friends and teams

Results are a great conversation starter. Ask a friend to take the test too, then compare what you each want most and what tends to stress you out. You may discover why one of you needs a plan while the other needs space, or why someone always speaks up first in a group, and that nobody is doing it wrong.

Teams sometimes use the Enneagram to talk about different working styles. Keep it light and curious: use types to understand each other better, never to judge, rank or box anyone in. Try one growth tip from your result page this week and see how it feels.
