Spin the Wheel: a fair random picker for any decision

Updated October 9, 2026

When a group cannot decide where to eat, who goes first or who does the dishes, a spinning wheel ends the debate in a few seconds. This guide explains how the wheel works, how to use weighted odds, and how to run it for lunch, classrooms and party games.

What the wheel does

You type a list of options, and the tool draws them as slices of a colorful wheel. Press spin and the wheel turns for a few seconds, slows down and stops on one slice, which is announced as the winner. You can use anywhere from two to sixteen options, and each label can be up to twenty-four characters long. Long names shrink or are shortened to fit narrow slices.

The wheel is designed to be quick to set up. Several ready-made lists, such as lunch ideas, dares, names, yes or no and numbers one to ten, let you start with one tap and edit from there. You can shuffle the order, choose between a few color themes, switch sound effects on or off, and see a short history of recent spins.

How to use it, step by step

A typical spin takes less than a minute from the first option to the final result.

  • Type your options or start from a quick preset, then edit the list.
  • If some options should be more likely, turn on weighted odds and raise their numbers.
  • Press spin and watch the wheel slow down on its own.
  • Spin again, or remove the winner and spin again to pick several people in turn.

Good ways to use a wheel

Food is the classic case. Put six or eight places or dishes on the wheel and accept whatever lands, because the real goal is to stop the endless "I do not mind, you choose" loop. If everyone secretly dislikes the result, you will find out at once, and you have lost only a few seconds.

In a classroom or a meeting, the wheel is a neutral way to choose who answers, presents or goes first. Because the wheel decides, nobody feels singled out. The remove-and-spin-again button is ideal here: each name drops off after it is chosen, so everybody gets a turn and nobody is picked twice. The same trick works for assigning chores, ordering players in a board game or picking a movie from a shared list.

For games and parties, fill the wheel with dares, questions, topics for a quiz or prizes. Keep entries short so that they stay readable on the slices, and make a separate wheel for each round or category. The numbers preset is also handy as a digital die when you have misplaced the real one.

Fairness and weighted odds

The winner is drawn at random the moment you press spin, using your browser's cryptographic random generator, and the animation then rotates the wheel so that it stops on that slice. Tapping at a clever moment or watching the wheel closely changes nothing. When odds are equal, every option has the same chance on every spin, no matter what came up before. Streaks and repeats are normal for random choices and do not mean the wheel is broken.

Weighted odds let you make some options more likely on purpose. Each option gets a weight from one to five, and its chance equals its weight divided by the total of all weights. With weights of two, one and one, the first option wins half of the time and the other two win a quarter each. Higher weights also make the slice visibly wider, so everyone can see the odds at a glance. This is useful for raffles where people hold different numbers of tickets, or for a family vote where one favorite dish should appear more often.

A short history of leaving things to chance

People have used chance to settle disputes for thousands of years. Drawing lots was a common way to choose officials in ancient Athens, where it was seen as protection against favoritism, and many cultures used lots to divide shares fairly. The wheel itself is also an old symbol: medieval art showed the goddess Fortuna turning a great wheel that raised some people and lowered others.

Modern game shows, arcade wheels and board game spinners carry the same idea into entertainment. The appeal is simple. A visible wheel makes the randomness feel open and fair, and a few seconds of suspense turns an argument into a small event that everyone can enjoy together.

Sharing and privacy

You can share your wheel with a link. The options, weights and color theme are encoded in the address itself, so nothing is stored on a server, and whoever opens the link sees the same wheel ready to spin. For your own convenience, the last wheel you built is kept in your browser, so avoid typing sensitive information as options if you use a shared computer. The tool works in any phone, tablet or desktop browser, with no download and no account.
