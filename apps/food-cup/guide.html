Food Tournament: how to crown your favorite dish

Updated October 9, 2026

A food tournament turns a vague question, what is my favorite food, into a series of easy duels. You never have to rank sixteen dishes at once. You only choose between two, again and again, until one is left standing. This guide explains how the tournament works, how to get more out of it, and where the bracket idea comes from.

How the tournament works

Sixteen dishes are shuffled into a bracket. Each match shows two of them side by side. Tap the one you would rather eat right now, and it moves on while the other is knocked out. On a computer you can also use the left and right arrow keys.

The bracket has four rounds: the round of 16 with eight matches, the quarterfinals with four, the semifinals with two and the final with one. That makes fifteen picks in total. At the end you see your champion dish and the four dishes that reached your semifinals. Every round starts with a new random bracket, so no two tournaments are the same.

  • Look at the two dishes and trust your appetite.
  • Tap the one you would rather eat.
  • Keep going through the quarterfinals and semifinals.
  • Crown your champion in the final and share it.

Real percentages, never invented ones

After you choose, the tournament can show how many other players made the same pick in that exact matchup. These numbers are real. Each pick is counted anonymously on the server, once per browser for each matchup, so replaying does not inflate the totals. A percentage appears only when enough people have played that duel. Until then you see nothing rather than a made-up number.

The same goes for the champion. You may see how many players also crowned your winner, but only once there is a meaningful number of finished tournaments. If the counting service is unavailable, the game simply plays without percentages.

Why head-to-head choices work

Ranking a long list is tiring and often unreliable, because your mind keeps changing the criteria. Choosing between two items is much easier. Comparing options in pairs is an old idea in the study of voting and ranking, and tournaments built on it feel quick and satisfying because every decision is small.

There is one thing to keep in mind. A single-elimination bracket is influenced by luck of the draw. If your top two favorites meet in the first round, one of them goes home early. That is part of the fun, and it is also why the result is a snapshot of how you feel in this particular run, not a final ranking. Play again for a different path.

Tips for better picks

Decide what your question is before you start. Are you choosing what you would eat tonight, what you love most overall, or what you would share with a friend? Mixing criteria makes the picks feel inconsistent.

  • Stay with one question for the whole run, such as what you would order right now.
  • Imagine the dish in front of you for a moment before tapping.
  • Do not overthink early rounds. Save your careful thinking for the semifinals.
  • Replay a few times. Dishes that keep reaching your last four are your real favorites.
  • Notice which dish surprised you by winning.

Playing with friends, and where tournaments come from

The bracket idea is familiar from sports, and online it became popular as a way to rank favorite foods, characters and songs. Korean web users enjoyed one well-known version under the name ideal type world cup, using the same one-on-one elimination. A food version suits groups well, because choosing a restaurant is a real problem that people face every week.

Try it with friends or colleagues. Everyone plays a round, shares the champion, and compares. If two people crown the same dish, you have found tonight's dinner. If not, play for a tiebreaker or use the coin flip tool. Arguing about the winner is half the entertainment.
