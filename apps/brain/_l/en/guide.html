🧠Left or Right Brain Test

Left brain right brain test guide: the myth, the science and how to play

Updated November 8, 2026

Are you a careful planner who loves lists, or someone who follows a hunch and sketches ideas on napkins? The Left Brain Right Brain Test turns those everyday habits into a light-hearted quiz of twelve questions. Before you start, one honest note: the idea that each person is ruled by one half of the brain is a popular myth. Real brains use both hemispheres together all the time. This quiz is just for fun and only looks at thinking-style preferences, using “left-brain style” and “right-brain style” as friendly nicknames.

What this quiz is and how it works

The quiz asks twelve short questions about ordinary situations: choosing something new to buy, spending a free weekend, giving directions, helping a friend with a decision or learning a new game. Each question has four answers and none of them is right or wrong. It takes about two minutes, and you can tap the back arrow if you change your mind.

Behind the scenes, every question looks at one of three thinking habits. The first is how you decide: do you reason it out step by step, or trust a feeling? The second is how you organize time: do you plan ahead, or go with the flow? The third is how you explain and remember: with words and sentences, or with pictures and space? Four questions look at each habit, and each answer has a stronger and a softer version, so you can lean a little or a lot.

The combination of the three habits leads to your brain character. The scoring was checked with every possible combination of answers, so random answers land on every result equally often, and the same answers always give the same result. Your result page also shows a left/right bar. It is calculated only from your own answers: choices for logic, plans and words fill the left-brain style side, choices for intuition, flow and images fill the right-brain style side.

Where the left brain, right brain idea came from

The story starts with real science. In the nineteenth century, doctors such as Paul Broca and Carl Wernicke noticed that damage to certain areas on the left side of the brain often affected speech and language. That was one of the first clues that the two hemispheres are not identical.

The big breakthrough came in the 1960s. Some patients with severe epilepsy had surgery that cut the corpus callosum, the thick bundle of fibers that connects the two hemispheres. Psychologist Roger Sperry and his student Michael Gazzaniga ran clever experiments with these split-brain patients. When a picture was shown only to the right hemisphere, many patients could not name it out loud, yet their left hand could pick out the matching object. The left hemisphere seemed to handle most language, while the right was better at some spatial and visual tasks, such as recognizing faces or copying shapes. Sperry received a Nobel Prize in 1981 for this work.

Gazzaniga later described the left hemisphere as an “interpreter” that quickly invents explanations for what we do, even when it lacks information. Those findings were fascinating, but they were about people whose hemispheres could no longer talk to each other, and about specific tasks measured in the lab.

Why the dominance myth spread, and what science says now

In the 1970s and 1980s, magazines, self-help books and business courses turned the lab results into a simple story: logical, organized people are “left-brained,” and creative, emotional people are “right-brained.” It was easy to remember and fun to talk about, so it spread quickly, even into schools and job seminars.

Modern research paints a different picture. Brain imaging shows that almost every activity, from reading a story to solving a math problem or painting a picture, uses networks spread across both hemispheres. Language and creativity both rely on teamwork between many regions. A well-known study from the University of Utah looked at brain scans of more than a thousand people and found no evidence that individuals are generally left-brain or right-brain dominant. Some functions are lateralized, which means one side does a bit more of the work, but people are not divided into two brain types.

So what does this quiz measure? Not your brain hemispheres. It measures thinking-style preferences: habits you lean toward in daily life, such as planning or improvising, reasoning or trusting your gut, and words or images. Those habits can change with mood, age, culture and practice, and most people switch between them depending on the situation.

What thinking styles really mean

Thinking styles describe how you like to approach things, not how smart or talented you are. A person who loves plans can still be very creative, and someone who follows intuition can still be excellent at math. Styles are preferences, a bit like being left-handed or right-handed for certain tasks while still using both hands every day.

  • Answer the way you usually are, not the way you think you should be.
  • Picture an ordinary week, not an unusual one when everything went differently.
  • If two answers both fit, choose the one you would pick on a relaxed day.
  • A balanced bar is not boring. It means you switch styles easily.
  • Try stretching your less-used style on purpose. That is where new ideas often come from.

Playing with friends, family and coworkers

The quiz is made for sharing. Send the link to a group chat and ask everyone to post their result and their left/right bar. Before anyone looks, try to guess who will lean toward plans and who will lean toward flow. Teams at work or at school can use it as an icebreaker: people with different styles often make the best partners, because one keeps the project on track while the other brings fresh ideas.

Your result page also shows a best buddy and a friendly rival. Your best buddy shares your habits for time and expression but decides in the opposite way, so together you cover both logic and intuition. Your friendly rival sees things differently on every side, which can lead to fun debates about whether the trip needs a schedule or just a full tank and a good playlist.

Just for fun

This quiz is entertainment. It is not a psychological test, it has not been scientifically validated, and it cannot diagnose anything or describe how your brain actually works. The left-brain and right-brain labels are a playful way to talk about habits, nothing more. Enjoy your brain character, compare notes with friends, and remember that your whole brain is always on your team.
