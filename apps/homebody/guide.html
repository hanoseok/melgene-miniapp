Homebody Test: how homebody are you, and what does it mean?

Updated October 21, 2026

The homebody test is a free, one-minute quiz that turns ten everyday situations into a homebody percentage and a short portrait of your style. This guide explains what a homebody is, how the score is built, how to read your number, and how to use it for fun and a little self-understanding.

What is a homebody?

A homebody is someone who is happiest spending free time at home. The word is old and friendly: it describes a person who enjoys their own space, their own routines and the comfort of familiar things, not someone who is afraid of the world. In Korea people say jipsuni and jipdori for homebody women and men, and in Japan the word indoor-ha describes the same preference, so the idea is clearly not tied to one culture.

Being a homebody is a preference, not a flaw and not a diagnosis. Plenty of homebodies have busy social lives; they simply like to keep the number of outings small and to choose them carefully. Others go out all the time and still describe their home as the place where they truly recharge. The quiz treats the home side and the outdoor side as two equally valid ways to live.

How the homebody test works

You need about a minute and no account. The test describes ten ordinary situations, such as a spontaneous invitation or a free weekend, and gives you four possible reactions each.

Every answer carries a point value from 0 to 10, where a higher value means a stronger pull toward home. Your ten values are added up and stretched onto a 0 to 100 scale, so you get a single homebody percentage. That percentage then falls into one of six types, each with its own short portrait, strengths and a best-match type.

  • Read each situation and picture something that really happened to you recently.
  • Choose the reaction you would have today, not the one you think sounds more interesting.
  • Use the back arrow if you want to change an earlier answer.
  • After the last answer, wait a moment for your percentage, your type, your strengths and how others may see you.
  • Share the result page if you like; friends who open the link can take the same test.

How to read your percentage

A low percentage means you tend to get your energy from being out and about, and a high percentage means you tend to get it from your own space. Neither end is better. The middle is perfectly normal, too: many people sit in the middle and move toward one side depending on the week.

If you finish the test, your result page may also show how you compare with other players. That line appears only when at least twenty finished tests have been counted anonymously, and it reflects the real numbers rather than an estimate. Until enough people have played, the line stays hidden instead of showing an invented figure. Because the score depends on how you feel today, a busy month, a new job or a tired week can move it up or down.

Homebody, introvert or just tired?

People often mix up three different ideas. Introversion and extroversion describe where you get your energy: from quiet and solitude, or from company and stimulation. Being a homebody describes where you like to spend your time. Social anxiety is something else again, a persistent fear of being judged that can make going out distressing. You can be an extroverted homebody who loves hosting friends on the couch, or an introverted wanderer who walks alone for hours.

Research on solitude suggests that time alone can restore mood and creativity when you choose it, while long periods of isolation that you did not choose can feel lonely and heavy. A good rule is to notice how you feel after your quiet time. If you feel recharged and content, you are enjoying your home. If you feel stuck or low, a short walk or a call with a friend may help, and if the feeling lasts, it is worth talking to someone you trust or a professional.

Fun only: using and sharing your result

This test is for entertainment and conversation. It is not a psychological assessment and cannot say anything reliable about your personality, health or relationships. Try taking it with friends or a partner and compare your percentages, then talk about where the descriptions fit and where they do not. The best-match and clashing sections are playful prompts about what kind of weekend partner balances you out, not predictions about compatibility.
