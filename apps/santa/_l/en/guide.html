Secret Santa: how the gift draw works and how to organize it

Updated October 28, 2026

Secret Santa is a simple idea that makes a big group feel close: instead of everyone buying something for everyone, each person draws one name and buys a single, thoughtful gift for that person, without telling anyone. On exchange day the gifts are handed out and people try to guess who their Santa was. This guide explains how the draw works on one phone, how to organize a smooth exchange, how much to spend, how to keep couples or housemates from drawing each other, how to run it when people are far apart, and a little of the history behind Wichteln, Secret Santa and the Korean Manito.

How the draw works

Type in everyone who is taking part, from 3 to 30 people. You can add an event name, a budget and an exchange date so everyone sees them when they look. When you press the draw button, the app shuffles the names on this phone and builds a chain where nobody has themselves. Then you pass the phone around. Each person taps their own name, confirms that it is really them, opens a little gift box to see who they are buying for, and hides it again before handing the phone on.

The full list of who drew whom stays hidden. The organizer can look at it, but only after a warning, because it spoils the surprise. If the group is not in one room, the organizer can make a personal link for each person instead. Each link opens only that one person’s gift box.

  • Type the names, one per line.
  • Optionally add the event name, budget, date and any pairs who should not draw each other.
  • Press the draw button.
  • Pass the phone: tap your name, confirm, open the box, then hide it.
  • When everyone has looked, the draw is complete.

Tips for organizing a smooth exchange

Most Secret Santa problems come from things nobody said out loud. Decide the basics before the draw, write them into the event name or a group message, and the rest takes care of itself. Give people at least two weeks between the draw and the exchange so nobody has to panic-buy, and remind everyone the day before.

Wish lists help a lot, especially in workplaces where people do not know each other well. A short note such as “I like tea, puzzles and anything green” turns a guess into a gift. Ask people to avoid jokes that could embarrass someone in front of colleagues.

  • Agree on the budget, the date and the place before drawing.
  • Collect short wish lists or a few likes and dislikes.
  • Set a rule for allergies, alcohol or anything people would rather not receive.
  • Keep the reveal relaxed: guessing is fun, but nobody has to confess.

Budget etiquette

A clear, equal budget is the single most important rule. It keeps the exchange fair for people with different incomes and saves the awkward moment when one person opens something expensive and another opens a keyring. Choose an amount that the least comfortable person in the group would happily spend, and treat it as a target, not a minimum. Going slightly under is fine; going far over makes others feel uneasy.

Homemade gifts, a favourite book or snack, or a small experience such as a coffee voucher all work well within a modest budget. If the group is mixed, say so in the event details so nobody has to ask.

Keeping couples apart and making the draw fair

Couples, siblings and housemates usually buy each other presents anyway, so drawing each other in Secret Santa feels like a wasted turn. Add them as a pair who should not draw each other, and neither of the two will get the other. You can also switch on the option that prevents two people from simply swapping gifts with each other, which makes the chain feel more like a real circle.

Fairness matters because people notice when the same friends keep drawing each other. This app uses the browser’s cryptographic random generator and reshuffles until every rule is met, so every valid arrangement is exactly as likely as any other. Be careful not to over-constrain a small group: with only three or four people and several excluded pairs, there may be no valid arrangement at all. In that case the app tells you before drawing instead of quietly breaking a rule.

Running a remote or online exchange

Families spread across cities and teams working from home can still play. Draw the names once, then use personal links: copy each person’s link and send it to them in a private message, not in the group chat. Ask everyone to open their link when they are alone. Agree on a shipping deadline that leaves time for the post, and collect addresses privately so nobody can work out who is sending what.

For the reveal, a short video call works well. Everyone opens their parcel on camera and tries to guess who sent it. Digital gifts such as an e-book or a streaming voucher are a good fallback for anyone who is hard to post to.

Wichteln, Secret Santa and Manito: a short history

Anonymous gift draws exist in many cultures. In German-speaking countries the custom is called Wichteln, after the Wichtel, a small helpful house spirit from folklore who does good deeds unseen. In northern Germany and Scandinavia there is also the Julklapp, where a wrapped present was traditionally thrown into the room with a knock on the door so the giver stayed unknown. The English name Secret Santa spread mainly through schools and offices in the twentieth century; nobody knows exactly where it started.

Spanish speakers call it amigo invisible or amigo secreto, and in Brazil the amigo secreto reveal is a party ritual in which each person describes their friend before saying the name. In Korea the game is known as Manito, a word usually traced to the Spanish manito, a friendly way of saying buddy or little brother. Korean Manito often lasts a week or more: you secretly do kind things for your person at school, on a club retreat or at work, and reveal yourself at the end. Whatever it is called, the spirit is the same, a small act of kindness made more fun by the secret.
