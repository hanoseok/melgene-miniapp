Halloween merge game: how to play and stack higher

Updated October 9, 2026

This is a drop-and-merge puzzle in the style of the Suika Game, dressed up for Halloween. You drop treats into a jar, matching pairs combine into something bigger, and the goal is to keep the pile under the line for as long as you can. Here is how it works and how to play it well.

How to play

A jar sits at the bottom of the screen and a small treat hangs at the top, ready to drop. You move the treat left and right to choose where it will land, then release it. When two identical pieces touch, they merge into a single piece of the next size up, and that new piece can touch another identical one and merge again. The preview of the next piece lets you plan one step ahead.

There is no timer. You play at your own pace until the pile gets too high. A row of the stages above the jar shows the order in which pieces grow, so you always know what a pair will turn into. Only the smaller pieces are handed to you, which is why the biggest ones can only be created by merging.

  • Touch screen: drag to aim, then lift your finger to drop.
  • Mouse: move to aim, click to drop.
  • Keyboard: left and right arrows or A and D to aim, Space, Enter or the down arrow to drop.
  • Eleven sizes in total, from the smallest candy up to a giant jack-o'-lantern.
  • Pause any time, and the game also pauses by itself if you switch tabs.

Strategy that works

Pick one corner of the jar and keep your biggest piece there. Build a rough staircase down from that corner, with each piece smaller than its neighbour. This keeps the pieces that can merge close together and stops large pieces from rolling into the middle and blocking small ones. Most lost games start when a big piece ends up in the centre.

Do not drop a piece the moment it appears. Take a second to look at the whole jar. Ask which pair is one drop away from merging, and where a piece will roll after it lands, because round pieces move. A drop that merges something but leaves a gap or a lopsided pile can be worse than a quiet drop that keeps things tidy.

Use the preview. If the next piece matches one on the surface, you can leave the current piece in a safe spot to set up a merge for the next drop. Avoid dropping small pieces onto big ones in the middle of the jar, because they tend to wedge into gaps and stop the larger merges from happening.

Scoring, chains and the end of a game

Every merge earns points, and bigger merges earn more. The score for creating a piece grows with its stage, so a high-stage merge is worth far more than several low ones. The most valuable moves are chain reactions, where a merge creates a piece that immediately touches a matching one and merges again, and again. Setting up a chain is the best way to turn a long game into a high score.

The game ends when the pile stays above the dashed line near the top of the jar for about two seconds. A piece that has only just been dropped or created is ignored for a moment so a normal drop does not end the game. In practice, that means you should never let the pile creep up to the line. When you are close, drop small pieces on the sides, not on top, and work on merging the ones you already have.

Where this kind of game comes from

Merge puzzles belong to a family of casual games in which you combine matching things to make something better. The Japanese game known as Suika Game, which means watermelon game, turned that idea into a physics toy where fruit tumbles and rolls, and it became a worldwide phenomenon. Similar versions spread widely in other countries too, and the Halloween edition here swaps fruit for candy, bats, ghosts and pumpkins.

The pumpkin theme has deep roots. Halloween grew out of the Celtic festival of Samhain, which marked the end of the harvest, and carved lanterns became part of the tradition. In Ireland people carved turnips and later, in North America, pumpkins turned out to be larger and easier to carve, which is how the jack-o'-lantern became the symbol of the season.

Playing with friends and sharing your score

A good way to play with friends is to trade the device after each game and see who stacks the highest, or have everyone play on their own device and compare. When a game ends, only your score is sent anonymously and compared with real scores from other players to show a top percentage. If there are not yet enough scores to compare with, or the connection fails, that part simply stays hidden.

Your best score is saved in your own browser. The game respects reduced-motion settings by turning off some of the merge effects, so it stays comfortable if flashing and movement bother you. Take it slowly, keep your biggest piece in the corner, and see how large a pumpkin you can grow.
