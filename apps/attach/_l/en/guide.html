Attachment style test guide: how we bond, and why it matters

Updated November 2, 2026

Why do some people feel calm when a friend goes quiet for a day, while others replay the last conversation again and again? Why do some of us long for closeness and then suddenly want space? Psychologists use the idea of attachment style to describe these patterns. The Attachment Style Test turns that idea into a short, friendly quiz built from everyday moments with friends, family and partners. It is made for fun and self-reflection, so treat your result as a mirror to think with, not a label to live by.

Where attachment theory comes from

Attachment theory began with the British psychiatrist John Bowlby in the 1950s and 1960s. Bowlby noticed that babies are born with a strong drive to stay near a caring adult, especially when they feel scared, tired or unwell. He described that adult as a safe haven to run to in distress and a steady base from which to go out and explore. When care is warm and reliable most of the time, a child learns that people can be trusted and that their own needs are acceptable.

The American-Canadian psychologist Mary Ainsworth tested these ideas in a famous study often called the Strange Situation. Toddlers spent a few minutes in an unfamiliar playroom, their parent briefly left and then returned, and the researchers watched what happened at the reunion. Some children were upset and quickly comforted, some barely seemed to react, and others clung and protested at the same time. Those different reunion patterns became the starting point for decades of research on how we bond.

From childhood to adult relationships

In the 1980s, researchers Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver asked a simple question: do adults show similar patterns in romantic love? Their answer was yes, and later work showed the same ideas apply to close friendships and family ties. Adults, like children, turn to the people they trust when life gets hard, and they react strongly when that connection feels threatened.

Importantly, early experience is not destiny. Your style can be shaped by many relationships over your life, it can look different with different people, and it can change. Psychologists talk about people becoming more settled over time through kind, consistent relationships, reflection and sometimes therapy.

The two-dimension model behind the quiz

Modern research usually describes adult attachment along two separate scales rather than as rigid boxes. The first is attachment anxiety: how much you worry about being rejected, forgotten or loved less than you love. The second is attachment avoidance: how uncomfortable you feel depending on others and letting them depend on you. Psychologist Kim Bartholomew showed that combining these two scales gives four broad patterns, and later questionnaires by Kelly Brennan, Catherine Clark and Phillip Shaver made the two-dimension approach the standard in research.

This quiz borrows that structure in a light, simplified way. Each answer nudges one or both scales a little, and your result is the area where your answers land. Because most people sit somewhere near the middle on at least one scale, it is completely normal to recognize yourself in parts of a result and not in others. On your result page you can also see where your answers leaned on each scale.

How to get the most honest result

The test has eleven everyday situations and takes about two minutes. There are no right or wrong answers, and no answer is more mature than another. The more honest you are, the more useful the reflection will be.

  • Answer with your first instinct, the way you actually react, not the way you wish you reacted.
  • Think of the relationships that matter most to you right now, whether friends, family or a partner.
  • If two options both fit, pick the one you do more often on an ordinary week.
  • Use the back arrow if you change your mind; nothing is saved until the end.
  • Retake it after a few months or a big life change and compare. Patterns can shift.

Just for fun: what this test is and is not

This quiz is entertainment and a prompt for self-reflection. It is not a psychological assessment, it has not been validated by researchers, and it cannot diagnose anything. Attachment style is not a mental health condition, and no style is bad. Each one is a way of coping that once made sense, and each comes with real strengths.

If relationships often feel painful, frightening or confusing, or if old experiences keep shaping your days, please consider talking with a licensed counselor or therapist. Professional, research-based questionnaires and a caring conversation can tell you far more than any online quiz.

Using your result with friends and partners

Results can be a gentle way to start a conversation. Share yours with a friend or partner and ask how they react when someone goes quiet, or what helps them feel close. You may discover why one of you texts right away while the other needs an evening alone, and that neither of you is doing it wrong.

Try turning the tips on your result page into small experiments: say what reassures you, name it when you need space, or stay a moment longer in a warm conversation. Small, repeated moments of trust are exactly how people grow more at ease with closeness, at any age.
