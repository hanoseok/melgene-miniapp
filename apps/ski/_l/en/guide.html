Ski game online: how to play and ski farther

Updated November 8, 2026

Ski Downhill is a simple endless ski game that runs in the browser on a phone, tablet or computer. You point your skis down a snowy mountain that never ends, weave around pine trees, rocks and snowmen, and slip between pairs of slalom flags for bonus meters. The longer you stay on your feet, the faster the slope gets. This guide explains how the controls and scoring work, shares habits that help you ski farther, and looks at the real sport that inspired the game: the history of slalom racing and the physics of a carved turn.

How to play

Press the start button and your skier waits at the top of the run. Nothing moves until your first input, so you can get comfortable first. Tap or press anywhere on the slope, or press an arrow key, and the run begins. From then on the mountain scrolls up past you while the skier stays in the upper part of the screen, which gives you a clear view of what is coming.

Steering is all about holding, not tapping. Hold the left half of the slope to drift left and the right half to drift right; let go and the skier straightens out. On a computer the ← and → keys (or A and D) do the same. The skier does not snap sideways: it leans into the turn and builds sideways speed smoothly, the way real skis bite into snow, so you need to start a turn a little before you reach an obstacle.

  • Distance is measured in meters and grows the whole time you are moving.
  • Two flags of the same color form a gate. Ski between them for a bonus; several gates in a row build a combo that makes each bonus larger.
  • Missing a gate never ends the run, but it resets your combo to zero.
  • Touching a tree, a rock or a snowman ends the run immediately. The edges of the slope are fenced, so you can lean on them safely.
  • If you switch tabs or lock your phone, the game pauses by itself and waits for you.

Tips for longer runs

Most crashes happen for one of two reasons: the turn started too late, or the skier was still sliding sideways when the next obstacle appeared. Because sideways speed builds and fades gradually, the best runs look calm. Small, early corrections beat big, last-second swerves almost every time.

  • Look ahead, not at your skier. Pick the open line two or three obstacles down the hill and drift toward it early.
  • Use short holds. A quick press nudges you a little; a long press carries you across the slope. Releasing in time is as important as pressing.
  • Stay near the middle when you can. It leaves room to escape in either direction.
  • Go for gates only when the path is clear. A combo is worth a lot, but no bonus is worth a crash.
  • As the speed climbs, plan one move at a time and avoid zigzagging for no reason.
  • Play a few short sessions instead of one long one. Your reactions are sharper when you are fresh.

Slalom: a short history of the flags

The word slalom comes from Norwegian: sla means a gentle slope and låm means the track left in the snow. Norwegian skiers in the nineteenth century raced through woods and around natural obstacles, and the idea of marking a winding course with poles grew out of those races. In the 1920s the British ski pioneer Arnold Lunn set some of the first modern slalom courses with pairs of flags in Mürren, Switzerland, turning the sport into a test of precision rather than raw downhill speed.

Today alpine racing has several disciplines. Downhill is the fastest, with few turns and very high speeds. Slalom is the most technical, with gates set close together so racers change direction constantly. Giant slalom and super-G sit in between. Real gates alternate between red and blue so racers can read the course at speed, which is why the flags in this game alternate colors too.

The physics of a carved turn

Why does a skier lean to turn instead of simply stepping sideways? Modern skis are narrower in the middle than at the tip and tail, a shape called sidecut. When a skier tips the ski onto its edge and presses it into the snow, the ski bends into an arc and the edge follows that curve, cutting a clean line instead of skidding. That is a carved turn. The faster you go, the more the skier must lean inward to balance the force pushing them out of the turn.

Skidding, on the other hand, scrubs off speed as the skis slide sideways. That is the trade-off the game imitates: turning hard costs you a little forward speed, while gentle steering keeps you fast. Real racers spend years learning to start each turn early and finish it smoothly so that they never have to fight their skis, and the same rhythm works on a phone screen.

Playing with friends

Because every run is a fresh, random course, an endless ski game makes an easy party challenge. Pass one phone around and let everyone take three runs, keeping only their best distance. Or play on your own devices and share the result link, which shows your distance and your ski level without giving away anything about the course.

For variety, try house rules: a round where only gate bonuses count, a round played with one thumb, or a relay where each player must beat the previous score. When the server is available, your result is also compared with other players' runs, so you can see where your best attempt stands.
