Personal color guide: undertones, daylight checks and seasonal palettes

Updated October 23, 2026

Some colors make your skin look smooth and your eyes bright, while others make you look tired even after a good night’s sleep. Personal color analysis tries to explain why. This guide covers where the seasonal idea comes from, what undertone, depth and clarity mean, how to check your own coloring at home with nothing more than a window and a mirror, and how to use a palette without turning it into a strict rulebook. The Personal Color Test is a playful self-check, so treat your result as a starting point to experiment with.

What seasonal color analysis is

Seasonal color analysis groups people by the natural colors of their skin, hair and eyes, then matches them with clothing and makeup shades that live in harmony with those colors. The idea grew out of color theory used by painters and was popularized for fashion in the second half of the twentieth century. It borrowed the names of the seasons because each season has a recognizable mood of light and color in nature.

Modern systems usually split each season into smaller groups, because two people can share the same undertone and still look very different. One may be pale and delicate, another dark and dramatic. Personal color became especially popular in Korea and Japan, where many beauty brands describe their products by tone, and it has spread widely on social media.

Undertone, depth and clarity

Undertone is the subtle hue under the surface of your skin. Warm undertones lean golden, peachy or olive. Cool undertones lean pink, rosy or bluish. Neutral undertones sit between the two. Undertone is not the same as how light or dark your skin is: very fair and very deep skin can both be warm or cool.

Depth, sometimes called value, describes how light or deep your overall coloring is, including hair and eyes. Clarity, sometimes called chroma, describes whether your coloring looks crisp and vivid or soft and blended. Someone with dark hair, bright eyes and clear skin often looks best in strong, clean colors. Someone whose hair, skin and eyes melt together usually shines in gentler, grayed shades. The test combines these three scales into one palette.

How to check your coloring in daylight

Lighting changes everything. Warm indoor bulbs make everyone look more golden, and cool office lights pull skin toward blue. Phone cameras and beauty filters adjust colors automatically too. For the most honest look, stand facing a window during the day, ideally when the sky is bright but the sun is not shining straight onto you.

  • Remove makeup or at least foundation and blush, and pull dyed hair away from your face.
  • Look at the veins on the inside of your wrist and compare them with a white sheet of paper.
  • Hold a pure white and an ivory fabric under your chin, then a gold and a silver item, and notice which one makes shadows softer.
  • Compare a vivid color and a dusty version of the same color. One will usually look like it belongs to your face, the other like it sits on top.
  • Ask a friend to watch with you. Other people often notice differences we miss in our own reflection.

How the Personal Color Test works

The test asks twelve short questions about things you can observe yourself: veins, how your skin reacts to the sun, which metals and neutrals flatter you, your natural hair and eye colors, contrast and the shades people compliment. Every answer nudges the warm-cool, light-deep and bright-muted scales a little. At the end, the strongest combination decides your palette.

The scoring is fixed, so the same answers always give the same result. We checked every possible combination of answers to make sure each palette can come up and none of them dominates. Your result page shows real color swatches you can screenshot, a few shades to go easy on and simple makeup, hair and jewelry ideas.

Using your palette, and what a quiz cannot do

A palette is a tool, not a rule. Start with the area closest to your face, such as tops, scarves, glasses and lipstick, because that is where color makes the biggest difference. Shades outside your palette still work in shoes, trousers and bags. If you love a color that is not on your list, try a version that is a little lighter, deeper, softer or brighter until it feels right.

This test is for fun. A professional analyst compares many fabric drapes on your face in controlled, neutral light and can see things a questionnaire cannot. Hair dye, a tan, medication, makeup and even your screen settings can shift what you see. If your result surprises you, retake the test on a different day in natural light, compare results with friends and keep whatever makes you feel most like yourself.
