Would You Rather: how to play, what the percentages mean, and ways to enjoy it

Updated October 9, 2026

Would You Rather is the simplest game in the world: two options, one choice. This guide explains how a round works here, how to read the crowd percentages honestly, why forced choices are so revealing and entertaining, and how to turn a round into a party game, an icebreaker or a classroom activity.

How a round of Would You Rather works

Each round gives you twelve questions in a row. Every question shows two options and you can only pick one. As soon as you choose, you see what percentage of other players picked each side, so you instantly know whether you are with the crowd or against it. You can choose from five themed packs, such as everyday dilemmas, love, work and school, food and more extreme choices, or take a random mix from all of them.

At the end you get a summary that shows how often you sided with the majority, and a short description of what your pattern of choices says about your decision style. You can share that summary with a friend. They get the same twelve questions, and when they finish they see how many of your picks they matched, which makes a perfect small challenge to send in a chat.

  • Choose a pack that fits your mood, or let the random mix surprise you.
  • Read the two options and pick the one you would actually choose. Tap Skip if a question does not work for you.
  • Check the percentages that appear right after you choose. They show how others split on that very question.
  • Use the back arrow if you want to revisit a previous question.
  • At the end, read your summary and share it with a friend to compare answers.

Tips for getting more out of it

The most important tip is to answer honestly and quickly. These games are built on trade-offs, and the fun comes from noticing which value wins when you cannot have both, such as comfort versus adventure, honesty versus kindness, or time versus money. If you spend a minute building loopholes, you lose the part of the game that teaches you something about yourself.

It also helps to resist overthinking the wording. A question is always a simplified situation: nobody is asking you to predict what you would do in a complicated real life, only which option feels less bad or more appealing in the moment. If a question really does not suit you, skip it rather than forcing an answer. And when the percentages surprise you, treat them as a conversation starter. Ask yourself why you landed where you did, and ask a friend to defend the other side. Arguing the option you did not choose is one of the best ways to understand your own reasoning.

Why forced choices are so revealing

The would you rather format is a forced choice: there is no middle option, so you must reveal a preference. In daily life we rarely have to rank our values like this, and the game makes it visible. The result is often a small surprise, for example discovering that you care about being understood more than being admired, or that you would give up convenience before giving up independence.

The percentages add a social layer. Psychologists describe the false consensus effect, our habit of assuming that most people share our views and choices. A game that shows the real split corrects that assumption in a harmless way. Sometimes you will find you are in a large majority, and sometimes your pick is rare. Neither outcome means you are right or wrong; it simply tells you how common a preference is among the people who play. Keep in mind that the numbers come from people who chose to play this game, so they are interesting but not a scientific survey of the whole population.

Playing with friends, family and classes

Would You Rather works almost anywhere because it needs nothing but a phone and a few minutes. On a road trip or in a waiting room, read a question aloud and have everyone answer at the same time, then explain their reasons. At a party, split the room into two sides and let people walk to the option they pick. In a classroom or a team meeting it is a friendly icebreaker: people share opinions on low-stakes dilemmas first, which makes it easier to speak up later.

For couples and close friends, the love and everyday packs are good for learning how the other person thinks. A house rule that keeps arguments fun is that everyone must pick a side, and nobody is allowed to mock another person's choice. Teachers and group leaders can choose a pack that suits the audience and skip anything that feels too personal for the group. If you play against a friend with the share link, you can compare how often your picks matched and see who knows the other better.

Real numbers, privacy and fair play

The percentages on this site are running totals of real picks made in the game, pooled across all language versions. If the totals cannot be loaded, you will only see your own pick, because we do not show made-up numbers. Each browser counts once per question, so replaying a question will not add a second vote.

The game needs no sign-up. The server stores only a total for each option, with no names, accounts or personal details, so nobody can see what you personally picked. The questions are for fun, and some of the extreme ones are deliberately uncomfortable; please treat all answers as a game and not as a statement about who you really are.
