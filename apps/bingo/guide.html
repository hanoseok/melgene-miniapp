Goal bingo card: how to plan a year you will actually enjoy ticking off

Updated October 30, 2026

A goal bingo card puts your plans for the year on one grid. Instead of a long list of resolutions that fades by February, every goal gets its own square, and each one you reach earns a stamp. Complete a full row, column or diagonal and you score a bingo. This guide explains how to build your card, how to write goals that work in a small square, why the format helps you keep going, and how to look back on it when the year ends.

How to make your bingo card

Start by choosing a board size. A 3×3 board holds nine goals and is a good choice if you want a short, focused year. A 4×4 board holds sixteen. The classic 5×5 board holds twenty-five squares, or twenty-four goals plus a free square in the centre, which is the format you will see most often on Instagram and TikTok at New Year.

Give the board a title, pick a colour theme, and type one goal per square. Tap a square in the grid to edit it, or use Next to move through them in order. If you run out of ideas, Fill with ideas drops suggestions into the empty squares; treat them as prompts and rewrite them so they fit your own life. Shuffle mixes the squares so that easy and hard goals are spread around the board. When every square is filled, tap Done to see your finished card.

  • Nothing you type is sent to a server; the image is drawn on your device.
  • Tap a square on the finished card to stamp it, and tap again to undo.

How to write goals that fit in a square

The best bingo goals are short, concrete and finishable. “Get healthier” never feels done, but “Run a 5K”, “Cook ten new recipes” or “Book a dentist check-up” each have a clear moment when you can stamp the square. If a goal is huge, split it into a milestone that could realistically happen this year.

Mix different sizes and areas of life. A good card has a few stretch goals, plenty of medium ones and some easy wins that you can stamp in January to build momentum. Spread goals across health, money, learning, relationships, fun and rest so that the board does not turn into a second to-do list for work.

  • Start with a verb: read, visit, learn, save, call, finish.
  • Add a number when it helps: 12 books, 3 trips, 1 new skill.
  • Include a few goals that are purely for joy, like a picnic or a concert.
  • Keep each square under about five words so it stays readable on the card.
  • Use the free centre square as a reminder that rest counts too.

Why the bingo format keeps you motivated

A plain list makes it easy to see what you have not done. A bingo grid shifts the focus to what you have done: every stamp is visible, and lines start to form long before the board is full. That small reward of seeing a line light up is a nudge to pick the next square, which is the same reason game designers use progress bars and streaks.

The grid also gives you choice. You do not have to work through goals in order; on a low-energy week you can stamp something easy, and on a good week you can chase the square that completes a line. Psychologists often point out that a sense of progress and a sense of control both help people stick with long-term goals, and a bingo card gives you a little of each.

You can also print the image on A4 or letter paper and pin it to the fridge, a desk or the inside of a notebook, then colour in squares by hand as you go.

Reviewing your year at the end

When December comes around, open your saved board and look at it honestly. Count your bingo lines and stamped squares, but also notice which goals you never touched. Sometimes that means a goal was too big; sometimes it means it simply stopped mattering, which is useful information too.

A quick year-end reflection makes next year’s card better. Ask yourself which stamped square you are proudest of, which goal surprised you, and what you would like more of next year. Then make a new board, keep the goals that still excite you, and replace the rest. A fresh card is a small ritual that turns the end of one year into the start of the next.
