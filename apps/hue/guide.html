Find the Different Color: how to play and see more shades

Updated October 31, 2026

Find the Different Color is a quick color sense game. A grid of tiles appears, every tile looks the same, and exactly one of them is a slightly different shade. Tap it and a new, bigger grid appears with an even smaller difference. You have 60 seconds to clear as many levels as you can. It runs in any browser on a phone or computer, needs no download and keeps your best score on your device. This guide explains the rules, shares habits that help you go further, and looks at why some shades are so much harder to tell apart than others. It is a game for fun, not a medical test.

How the game works

Each level shows a square grid. The first one is only 2×2, so the odd tile is easy to spot. As you clear levels the grid grows to 3×3, 4×4 and eventually 8×8, which means 64 tiles to scan. At the same time the color difference shrinks. Early on the odd tile is clearly lighter or darker; later it is only a hair away from its neighbors.

Every level picks a new random color, so you might go from a soft teal to a warm orange to a dusty violet. Very dark and very pale colors are left out on purpose, because tiny differences there are close to invisible on most screens. Only the brightness of the odd tile changes, while its hue and saturation stay the same as the others.

  • Tap or click the tile that looks different to clear the level.
  • A wrong tap takes 3 seconds off the clock and shakes the grid.
  • When time runs out, the odd tile of the last level is highlighted and you see your result.
  • Keyboard players can move with the arrow keys and press Enter to pick a tile.

Scoring and your color sense level

Your score is simply the number of levels you cleared before the timer hit zero. At the end you also see how many wrong taps you made, the largest grid you reached and a playful color sense title based on your score. If our server is reachable, your score is compared anonymously with other games so you can see roughly where you stand; if not, that part is simply hidden.

Because the colors are random, two games are never exactly alike. Some rounds land on hues that are easy for your eyes and screen, and others feel almost impossible. Play a few games before deciding what your real level is.

Tips for spotting the odd tile faster

The biggest mistake is staring hard at one tile after another. Your eyes adapt quickly to a color, and after a second or two everything starts to look the same. Instead, relax your gaze and look at the whole grid at once. The odd tile often pops out in your peripheral vision before you can say which one it is.

  • Turn your screen brightness up and switch off night mode or blue-light filters while you play.
  • Hold your phone straight in front of you. Tilting it changes how light and dark areas look on many screens.
  • Look at the edges between tiles. A slightly lighter tile makes its borders look a little sharper.
  • If nothing stands out, glance away for half a second and look back. A fresh look often finds it.
  • Don't tap randomly. Three wrong guesses cost nine seconds, which is usually more than one careful look.

How we see small color differences

Color vision starts with three kinds of cone cells in the retina. They respond most strongly to long, medium and short wavelengths of light, roughly reds, greens and blues. Your brain compares their signals to work out both how bright something is and what hue it has. We are especially good at noticing brightness changes, which is why this game varies lightness rather than hue.

Sensitivity is not the same everywhere in the spectrum. Many people find differences in greens and yellows easier to see than in deep blues, and a change that is obvious in one color can disappear in another. To keep levels fair, the game measures differences in a perceptual color space called OKLab, which is designed so that equal numbers look roughly equally different to the human eye.

Your screen matters too. Phones and monitors use different panels, color settings and brightness levels, so the same tiles really can look different on two devices. That is why scores are best compared on the same screen, and why a low score says more about the moment than about your eyes.

Play it with friends

The game is short enough to pass around at a table or in a group chat. Take turns on the same phone so everyone gets the same screen, then compare levels and color sense titles. After a game you can share your result with one tap, and your friends will see the page in their own language. If someone in your group is curious about real color vision, remind them that only an eye doctor can test it properly, and that this is just a fun challenge.
