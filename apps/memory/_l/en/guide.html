Memory Match Game: how to play and remember more cards

Updated October 16, 2026

The Memory Match Game is one of the oldest card games around: lay the cards face down, turn over two at a time and try to find every matching pair. This Halloween version swaps the playing cards for pumpkins, ghosts, bats and sweets, and adds a timer and a move counter so you can see your memory improve. This guide covers the rules, the three levels, simple techniques that help you remember more cards, where the game comes from and how to enjoy it with children and friends.

What the Memory Match Game is

Every card in the grid has exactly one twin somewhere else on the board. At the start all of them are face down, so you have no idea where anything is. You turn over one card, then a second. If the two pictures are the same, you have found a pair and both cards stay open. If they are different, you get a short look at them before they turn back over, and it is up to you to remember what you saw.

The round ends when every pair is open. Your result shows how long it took, how many moves you made and your accuracy. Because the cards and the pictures are shuffled fresh every round with a fair random shuffle, there is no layout to learn by heart. Each game is a new test of short-term memory.

How to play, step by step

Choose a level on the start screen and press Start. The board appears with every card face down, and the clock starts as soon as you flip your first card, so you can take a breath before you begin.

  • Easy is a 4 by 3 grid with 6 pairs, Normal is 4 by 4 with 8 pairs and Hard is 4 by 6 with 12 pairs.
  • Tap a card to turn it over, then tap a second card. Two matching pictures stay face up.
  • Two different pictures stay visible for a moment and then flip back. If you are quick, tapping the next card turns them back straight away.
  • One move means one attempt with two cards. Accuracy is the number of pairs divided by your moves, so a perfect game is 100%.
  • If you leave the page or switch apps, the timer pauses and the cards are covered. Tap the board to carry on.

Memory tips that actually help

Most people can hold only a handful of separate items in short-term memory at once. On the Hard board there are twenty-four cards, so trying to remember every card as a separate fact quickly overloads you. The trick is to organise what you see.

  • Use chunking. Instead of remembering “ghost at row two, column three”, link nearby cards into a little story, such as “the ghost sits next to the bat in the top corner”. One picture is easier to hold than two facts.
  • Track positions, not just pictures. Work through the board in a fixed pattern, for example row by row from the top left, so you always know which area you have already seen.
  • Open new cards first. When your first card shows a picture you have not seen before, use the second flip on an unseen card too. It costs nothing and reveals more of the board.
  • Go for a match the moment you know one. If your first card shows a picture you saw a few moves ago, go straight to where you saw it before you forget.
  • Say the names quietly in your head. Hearing “candy, moon, candy” uses a second kind of memory alongside the visual one.

A short history of Concentration

Matching games with face-down cards have been played for centuries, often with an ordinary deck where any two cards of the same rank count as a pair. In English the game is widely known as Concentration or Pairs; in Japan it is called shinkei suijaku, which roughly means “nervous breakdown” as a joke about how tiring it can be. In 1959 the German publisher Ravensburger released a picture version called Memory, and the name stuck in many countries.

The idea also reached television. The American game show Concentration, which first aired in 1958, combined a giant matching board with a rebus puzzle and ran for many years. Today the same simple rules appear in teaching materials, brain-training apps and party games, because anyone can learn them in a few seconds.

Playing with kids and friends

Memory matching is a great game for children because the rules are easy and young players often beat adults. Start with the Easy level, let a child name each Halloween picture out loud as it appears and celebrate every pair. It is a gentle way to practise attention, turn-taking and patience.

With friends, try a fair challenge: everyone plays the same level three times and you compare the best time or the fewest moves. Your best time for each level is saved in this browser only, and when you finish a round your rounded time is compared with the real times of other players on the same level, so you can see how your memory measures up.
