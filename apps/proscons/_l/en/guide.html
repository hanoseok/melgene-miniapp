Pros and cons list guide: how to weigh a decision

Updated November 17, 2026

A pros and cons list is one of the oldest decision tools there is, and still one of the most useful. Writing the reasons down moves them out of your head, where they tend to go round in circles, and onto a page where you can see them all at once. The Pros and Cons Scale adds one thing a plain list lacks: weight. Each point counts as 1, 2 or 3, and a balance scale tips as you go. This guide explains how the scale works, where the idea comes from, how to choose weights, and when a list should not have the last word.

How the scale works

Start by typing what you are deciding, or leave it blank. Then choose the kind of decision. "Should I do it?" is for a single option, such as taking a course or buying a car: pros go on one side of the scale and cons on the other. "A or B?" is for choosing between two options, such as two job offers or two cities. Each option gets its own pros and cons, and the cons of one option add weight to the other side, because a drawback of A is effectively a reason to pick B.

Add points in a few words, up to 60 characters each and 12 per list. Every point starts at weight 2; tap the dots to change it to 1 or 3. The scale moves as you type, so you can watch the balance shift. When you are ready, press Weigh it to see the verdict: which side wins, by how many points, and what share of the total weight it holds, along with the three heaviest reasons on each side.

  • Type your question (optional) and pick "Should I do it?" or "A or B?".
  • Add short points to each side; tap a topic chip if you are stuck.
  • Set each point’s weight from 1 to 3 by tapping the dots.
  • Press Weigh it to see the margin and the heaviest reasons.
  • Copy the link to ask a friend, or save the result as an image.

Benjamin Franklin’s moral algebra

In September 1772 the chemist Joseph Priestley wrote to his friend Benjamin Franklin for advice about a difficult career choice. Franklin did not tell him what to do. Instead he described his own method. He divided a sheet of paper into two columns, one for reasons in favour and one for reasons against, and over three or four days he added whatever came to mind. Then he estimated how heavy each reason was. When a reason on one side seemed to equal one on the other, he struck both out; when one reason balanced two on the other side, he struck out all three. What remained showed him where the balance lay.

Franklin called this moral or prudential algebra. He admitted the weights could never be exact, but he found that seeing everything together, and giving each item its own weight, made him less likely to take a rash step. The Pros and Cons Scale follows the same idea: a few days of noticing reasons, a weight for each, and a balance you can see.

How to use the weights

Weights stop a long list of small things from outvoting one big thing. Five minor annoyances should not automatically beat one reason that truly matters to you. A simple rule of thumb works well: 1 for nice-to-have details, 2 for things that clearly matter, and 3 for points you would struggle to live without.

If you find that everything is a 3, try ranking the points instead. Ask which one you would give up first, then lower it. Weights are also a good place to be honest about uncertainty: a benefit that is only possible, not likely, deserves less weight than one you can count on.

  • 1: a small plus or minus you would barely notice after a month.
  • 2: something that clearly affects your days, money or plans.
  • 3: a dealbreaker or a core value, the kind of point that keeps you up at night.

Tips for a clearer decision

A list is only as good as the reasons on it, so give yourself room to find them. The topic chips (cost, time, health, fun, people, stress, growth, risk) are there to jog your memory, not to tell you what matters. Your draft is saved on your device, so you can close the page and come back.

  • Sleep on it. Like Franklin, come back after a day or two; new reasons appear and some weights change once the first emotion settles.
  • Separate facts from feelings. Write "the commute is 50 minutes" and "I dread the commute" as separate points; both are valid, but they are different kinds of reasons.
  • Ask a friend. Send the link and let them look at your scale. Someone else often spots a missing point or a weight that looks too high.
  • Watch your reaction. If the verdict disappoints you, that disappointment is useful information about what you really want.

When not to rely on a list

A pros and cons list is a thinking aid, not a judge. Some decisions hinge on a single factor that outweighs everything else, such as safety, health or a firm deadline, and no number of small advantages should overrule it. Others depend on information you do not have yet; in that case the best next step is to find out more, not to add more points.

For medical, legal or financial choices, use the scale to organise your questions, then talk to a qualified professional. And when the scale ends in a tie, that is an answer too: both paths may be fine. A fair coin flip can help you notice which result you were hoping for.
