CPS test: how it works and how to click faster

Updated November 4, 2026

A CPS test measures how many times you can click in one second. CPS simply stands for clicks per second. Gamers use it to check their speed before PvP matches, students try it during breaks, and friends pass a phone around to see who has the fastest fingers. Our version runs in any browser on a phone, tablet or computer, with no download and no account. This guide explains how the test is scored, which test length to choose, which clicking techniques people use, and how to improve without hurting your hand.

How the CPS test works

Pick a test length of 1, 5 or 10 seconds and press the start button. A large click area appears. Nothing happens until you click or tap inside it: the timer starts with your very first click, and that click already counts. From then on, every click inside the area adds one to your total until the time runs out.

When the time is up, the area locks for a moment so that a click you were already making does not start a new round by accident. Then you see your result: your clicks per second with one decimal place, your total number of clicks, a speed badge and your personal best for that mode.

  • Your score is the number of clicks divided by the length of the test in seconds.
  • Only mouse clicks and taps inside the area count. Keyboard keys are ignored, because a held key repeats automatically.
  • On a touchscreen every finger that touches the area counts as its own click.
  • If you switch tabs or lock your phone during a test, that attempt is cancelled and you simply try again.

Choosing 1, 5 or 10 seconds

The 1-second test is a pure burst. There is no time to get tired, so it usually gives the highest number, but it is also the most random: a single extra click changes your score by a whole point. It is great for quick bragging rights.

The 5-second test is the standard and a good balance between speed and stamina. The 10-second test is the most honest measure of your real speed, because your hand starts to tire in the second half. Each mode keeps its own best score, and when the server is available your result is compared only with other players in the same mode.

Popular clicking techniques

How you hold the mouse or phone matters more than raw finger strength. These are the methods people use, from the easiest to the most advanced.

  • Normal clicking: one finger presses the button in a steady rhythm. Relaxed wrists click faster than tense ones.
  • Jitter clicking: you tense the forearm so the hand vibrates and the finger taps very quickly. It is fast but tiring, so keep sessions short.
  • Butterfly clicking: two fingers take turns on the same button. Each finger only needs half the speed, so the total can be much higher.
  • On phones: use two fingers or both thumbs and tap with the tips, close to the screen, instead of lifting your hand high.
  • Whatever you choose, stop if your hand or wrist hurts. A few short rounds beat one long painful one.

What counts as a good CPS

With normal clicking most people land somewhere around 5 to 8 clicks per second. Practised gamers often reach 8 to 10, and jitter or butterfly clickers can go higher still. In fast-paced games, especially block-building PvP, a higher click rate means more hits in a short exchange, which is why players like to track it.

There is also a ceiling. Results above 25 clicks per second are beyond what a human finger can do and usually come from auto-clickers or broken hardware, so we do not save or compare them. The percentage you see after a test comes only from real results sent by other players in the same mode. If the server cannot be reached, that part is simply hidden.

Practice tips and playing with friends

To improve, warm up with a couple of easy rounds, sit so your forearm rests on the desk, and keep your grip light. Try the same mode several times in a row and watch your average rather than one lucky score. Many people find that a short break between rounds helps more than grinding nonstop.

The CPS test is also a fun group challenge. Hand one phone around and give everyone a 5-second round, or let each person play on their own device and share the result link. The link opens the test in your friend's own language without your history, so they start fresh and try to beat you.
